About the course

Almost everyone has an opinion about where AI is going. Very few of those opinions are attached to a model you can write down, argue with, and check against data. This course is an attempt to fix that.

We invite students to think hard about the future growth and impact of AI using tools from economics, finance, statistics, and operations research. We start with the technical content you need to have an informed view — how frontier models are actually trained, why their capabilities scale predictably, and what a capability measurement is really measuring. From there the course is largely discussion and participation-based, working through the serious quantitative literature on takeoff, growth, and labor, and ending in a research project of your own.

You will not leave with a confident forecast. You should leave able to tell a rigorous argument from a compelling one, and able to build your own.

Questions we will try to answer:

  • How much is the world spending on AI, and what is it buying?
  • How fast is AI research progressing, and how would we know?
  • Under what conditions does automating AI R&D produce explosive growth?
  • Which industries get transformed first, and why do diffusion lags exist?
  • Under what conditions is a coordinated slowdown even possible from a game-theoretic perspective?

Course outline

Four parts, roughly one topic per week. Meeting dates will be filled in once the schedule is set; the readings listed are the ones each topic is built around.

Part 1

Foundations of frontier AI

Students should arrive with basic knowledge of deep learning and NLP. These weeks build the shared technical vocabulary the rest of the course assumes: how frontier models are actually made, why scaling is predictable, and what a capability measurement is really measuring.

Week1

The frontier model training pipeline#

lecture

Pretraining, mid-training, and RL — where the compute goes, where the data comes from, and which stage is currently the bottleneck.

Week2

Predictable scaling: scaling laws and compute-optimal training#

lecture

Loss-versus-compute power laws, the compute-optimal frontier, and what scaling laws do and do not license you to extrapolate.

Readings

  • Hoffmann et al., 2022. Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla)
Week3

Measuring capability: benchmarks and their pathologies#

lectureguest

Saturation, contamination, construct validity, and the gap between benchmark scores and economically useful work. Introduces METR's task-horizon methodology as an alternative measurement strategy.

Readings

  • METR, 2025. Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks
  • Aubakirova, Atallah, Clark, Summerville & Midha, 2026. State of AI: An Empirical 100 Trillion Token Study with OpenRouter

Potential guest speaker from a frontier lab.

Part 2

Forecasting methods and economic models

The technical toolkit needed to reproduce, rather than merely read, the quantitative claims in this literature: time-horizon extrapolation, growth models with automation, and the core mechanics of a recursive self-improvement model.

Week4

Extrapolation and uncertainty: rebuilding the METR time-horizon curve#

lecture

Fitting doubling times, propagating uncertainty honestly, and the standard ways trend extrapolations mislead. Hands-on with the data.

Readings

  • METR, 2025. Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks
  • Epoch AI. GATE: Modeling the Trajectory of AI and Automation
Week5

Growth models with automation#

lecture

Ideas production functions, task-based automation, and the substitution elasticities that decide whether automation raises the growth rate or merely the level.

Readings

  • Aghion, Jones & Jones. Artificial Intelligence and Economic Growth
  • Erdil & Besiroglu, 2023. Explosive Growth from AI Automation: A Review of the Arguments
Week6

Recursive self-improvement: the core model#

lectureguest

The conditions under which automating AI R&D feeds back into itself, and the parameter values that separate an explosion from a fizzle.

Readings

  • Davidson, Halperin, Houlden & Korinek, 2026. When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth?
  • Elasticity Institute. Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement

Potential guest speaker from the takeoff-modeling literature (Forethought, Epoch AI, METR).

Part 3

Macroeconomic impact

Seminar format. Students are divided into groups; each group teaches the rest of the class the key ideas in an assigned reading — what the model assumes, what actually drives the result, and where it is fragile.

Week7

Explosive growth: the case and the objections#

seminar

Readings

  • Erdil & Besiroglu, 2023. Explosive Growth from AI Automation: A Review of the Arguments
  • Davidson, Halperin, Houlden & Korinek, 2026. When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth?
  • Epoch AI. AI in 2030
Week8

Labor, capital, and the long run#

seminar

If AI can do the work, what is human labor for, and who owns the returns?

Readings

  • Trammell. Is Labor a Luxury in the Long Run? Forethought
  • Trammell & Patel. Capital in the 22nd Century (excerpts)
  • Hall. The Politics of Jobless Prosperity
Week9

What markets believe: interest rates and existential risk#

seminar

If transformative AI were coming, would asset prices already show it? And what does a growth-versus-risk tradeoff look like when written down.

Readings

  • Chow, Halperin & Mazlish. Transformative AI, Existential Risk, and Real Interest Rates
  • Jones. The A.I. Dilemma: Growth versus Existential Risk
Week10

The labor market so far: evidence, not forecasts#

seminar

What we can actually measure today about exposure, adoption, and employment.

Readings

  • Eloundou et al., 2023. GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of LLMs
  • Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, 2025. Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of AI
  • Chatterji et al.. How People Use ChatGPT
Part 4

Applications, sector by sector

From the macro angle to the micro. For each sector: what current models can and cannot do, and why diffusion lags capability. Closes with scenario synthesis and final presentations.

Week11

Software, science, and silicon#

seminarguest

Software engineering, drug design, materials science, and chip design — the sectors where automation is furthest along, and the measurement problem of telling real speedups from perceived ones.

Readings

  • METR, 2025. Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity

Potential guest speaker from a frontier startup (e.g. Periodic Labs).

Week12

Law, finance, and defense#

seminar

Regulated and adversarial sectors, where the binding constraint is rarely raw capability.

Week13

Scenario synthesis#

seminar

We put the scenarios side by side — fast takeoff, slow transformation, and normal technology — and ask what evidence would move us between them.

Readings

  • Kokotajlo et al.. AI 2027
  • AI 2040
  • Narayanan & Kapoor. AI as Normal Technology

Final project presentations#

Prerequisites

This course is intended for graduate and advanced undergraduate students. Students should have a foundation in calculus, linear algebra, probability, algorithms, and machine learning. Students should be comfortable reading research papers and picking up any missing background.

Grading

40%

Attendance and participation

This is a discussion course. Showing up prepared, having read the week's material, is most of the work.

25%

Seminar presentation

In groups, you teach the rest of the class the key ideas in an assigned reading — the model, the assumptions that drive its conclusions, and where you think it breaks.

35%

Final project

See Final project.

Guest speakers

We aim to host several guest speakers from frontier labs, forecasting organizations, and startups. Confirmed speakers will be listed here as they are scheduled.

Final project

The final project is an original piece of analysis on some aspect of AI's trajectory or its economic impact. Strong projects typically do one of:

  • Replicate and stress-test. Reproduce a published forecast or growth model, then vary the assumptions that do the most work and report what breaks.
  • Measure something. Build a dataset — spending, compute, deployment, task horizons, prices, job postings — and use it to answer a question nobody has cleanly answered.
  • Model a sector. Take one industry and model the gap between what current systems can do and what actually gets adopted, with the diffusion lag made explicit.

Deliverables: a short proposal mid-semester, a conference-style write-up, and a presentation in the final weeks. Groups of one to three. Details will be posted here.
